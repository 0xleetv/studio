GEOMETRY 01

A coincidence the size of the sky

The sun is about 400 times wider than the moon, and on average about 400 times farther away. Two unrelated accidents of scale cancel out, so both discs measure roughly half a degree across our sky.

Total eclipses exist because, right now, that match is close enough. It does not have to be. Drag the moon along its orbit and watch the verdict flip.

GEOMETRY 02

Two shadows, one dark spot

The moon casts a double shadow. The penumbra is the wide, pale outer cone: stand in it and the sun is only partly eaten. The umbra is the dark inner cone, and on the ground it is tiny, never wider than about 270 kilometres.

Everything dramatic happens inside that dot. It sweeps the ground at roughly 1,700 to 5,000 kilometres per hour, slowest near midday, fastest at the edges of the path, so totality lasts longer near the middle.

GEOMETRY 03

Seven minutes, at best

Even perfectly placed, totality is short. The theoretical ceiling is about 7 minutes 32 seconds, and it takes a rare combination of moon size, sun distance and path geometry to approach it.

The longest total eclipse of the twentieth century, over Karachi in 1955, reached 7 minutes 8 seconds. Most are far shorter. Here is how recent ones compare.

LIGHT 04

The corona paradox

The sun's visible surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. Its outer atmosphere, the corona, runs past a million degrees, hotter than the surface it wraps. Working out where that heat comes from took most of a century; the answer is the sun's magnetic field.

You will never see it on a normal day. The sky's glare drowns it out. Totality, when the moon blocks the glare, is the only time the corona is visible to the naked eye, and it is what the dark sky in the opening scene is showing you.

LIGHT 05

The last bead

Seconds before and after totality, the last slivers of sunlight pour through the valleys and craters on the moon's ragged limb, strung out as points of light: Baily's beads, described by Francis Baily in 1836.

The single brightest bead, flaring beside the corona, is the diamond ring. It is the flash that ends the opening scene, and it happens because the moon's edge is mountains, not a polished circle.

FUTURE 06

The clock is running

Lunar laser ranging, bouncing beams off reflectors left on the moon, shows it drifting away at 3.8 centimetres per year. The size match between the two discs is slowly unwinding.

In roughly 600 million years the moon will be too small to cover the sun, and total eclipses will end. Until then the pattern repeats in families: every 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours, the saros cycle returns the same geometry, landing about a third of the way around the Earth.
